Abstract
This preprint summarizes a geometry-native routing program for sparse neural computation. The router maps
normalized token embeddings onto a single-shell hyperspherical manifold and partitions that manifold into
angular sectors derived from the Hopf base. Across the internal experiment chain summarized here, the
effective routed footprint grows sublinearly with routing capacity K, approximately as effective_buckets(K) =
2.957 * K^0.572 in the canonical static regime. The measured advantage is norm-invariant across L1/L2/L3/L4
normalization, degrades when radial shells are forced, and carries through to matched-progress compute
savings and hardware proxy savings. The resulting working theory is that a router constrained to a normalized
angular manifold can organize tokens into sectors whose occupancy grows sublinearly with routing capacity,
yielding fewer active routing regions, better locality, and lower memory traffic than a permuted control. The
paper presents the architecture, related work, internal methodology, the experimental chain to date, and the
design implications of the frozen law.
Status note. This document is an internal preprint-style synthesis of the projects current theory and experimental results. It is
intended for technical review, not as a claim of final external validation.

1. Introduction
Sparse routing has become a central strategy for scaling neural systems without paying the full cost of dense
computation. Modern mixture-of-experts systems, sparse attention systems, and retrieval-augmented architectures
all try to restrict computation to a task-relevant subset of the full parameter or memory space. Classic
conditional-computation work and later sparse expert models established the appeal of activating only a small
subset of modules for each example, but practical systems still struggle with routing overhead, locality, and load
balancing. Shazeer et al. introduced large-scale sparse expert activation, Fedus et al. simplified top-1 expert routing
in Switch Transformers, and Roy et al. showed that routing-based sparse attention could reduce nominal attention
complexity. Hyperbolic and Poincare-based representation work, meanwhile, demonstrated that geometry itself can
act as an inductive prior for structured representations. This project asks a different question: can geometric
structure itself become the routing mechanism, rather than merely the embedding prior?
The present router family was initially framed in terms of hyperbolic routing, Hopf structure, event gates, and
hardware reduction. Through the experiment chain summarized here, that story was progressively simplified. The
current working picture is cleaner than the original one: the main sparsity effect does not appear to require explicit
per-sample event gating, and it does not appear to be driven by a radial shell hierarchy. Instead, the dominant signal
is angular. Once embeddings are normalized onto a hypersphere, routing behaves like a sector-allocation process
on the Hopf base, and the effective number of active routing buckets grows sublinearly with bucket capacity K. The
result is a measurable reduction in active routing footprint, matched-progress routed computation, and hardware
proxy cost.
This document has four goals. First, it places the work in context relative to prior sparse-routing and
geometry-aware literature. Second, it states the current architecture and frozen canonical law as clearly as possible.
Third, it records the experimental chain and the quantitative results that support that law. Fourth, it distills the
design implications for future large-scale routing experiments and eventual integration into a real trainable model.
Main contributions of the current preprint stage:
 A concrete routing architecture in which normalized embeddings are routed on an angular manifold rather than
 through explicit learned gating logits.
 A measured effective-footprint law showing sublinear growth of active routing regions with routing capacity K in
 the strongest angular regime.
 A mechanism audit showing that the effect is angular, norm-invariant, and not improved by forced radial shells.
 
 A matched-progress compute analysis and a hardware proxy closure linking routing concentration to lower
 effective routing cost, fewer cache misses, and lower bytes moved.
 A frozen design rule: normalized angular routing is the current default architecture; shell hierarchies are not part of
 the default design under normalized embeddings unless future evidence justifies reintroducing them.
2. Positioning relative to prior work
The broader context includes at least four relevant lines of work. The first is conditional computation and sparse
expert routing. Sparse gating mechanisms for deep networks and mixture-of-experts layers were explored before
the modern large-language-model era, and sparsely gated MoE layers later demonstrated that conditional activation
could increase capacity without proportional compute growth. Switch Transformers simplified expert selection to
top-1 routing, explicitly highlighting that routing overhead and implementation details are part of the real systems
problem. These systems, however, typically compute gating decisions in Euclidean feature space and then treat
routing as a learned categorical choice rather than as a geometric consequence of the representation itself.

The second line is sparse attention and routing-based attention. Routing Transformer, for example, used online
clustering to reduce effective attention complexity. That work is relevant because it already treats routing structure
as part of the complexity story. The present work is different in that the central object is not a clustering algorithm
imposed on top of the representation; it is a normalized angular routing manifold that is intended to act as the
routing substrate itself. ■cite■turn552712search2■
The third line is geometry-aware representation learning, especially hyperbolic and Poincare-based methods.
Hyperbolic geometry has repeatedly been shown to encode hierarchical and relational data efficiently. The current
project was initially inspired by that general idea, but the current mechanism turns out not to rely on a multi-shell
hyperbolic hierarchy in the default normalized regime. Instead, it uses the angular component of the geometry and
an associated Hopf-style partitioning as the active routing mechanism. In that sense, the work is geometry-native
but not simply another hyperbolic embedding paper. ■cite■turn552712search3■
The fourth line is systems-oriented work on hardware locality. Standard sparse architectures often recover
theoretical gains only partially because irregular routing harms memory locality and introduces cache and
communication penalties. The significance of the current experiment chain is that it does not stop at a sparsity
claim; it also tests whether the measured routing concentration propagates into simple hardware proxy models.
That bridge, while still preliminary, is what makes the results interesting beyond abstract routing theory.
3. Current architecture and working theory
The current architecture can be described without unnecessary mysticism. Let x be a token representation in R^d.
Before routing, x is normalized onto a unit sphere under the chosen norm. In the default router family, L2
normalization is used:
 x_hat = x / ||x||_2
Because ||x_hat||_2 = 1 for all routed tokens, the router operates on a single-shell manifold. In practice that means
radial shells are absent by construction and routing must be driven by angular coordinates. The earlier event-gate
framing was valuable as an exploratory intuition, but the measured effect chain indicates that explicit per-sample
event gating is not the dominant sparsity mechanism. Sparsity is effectively baked into bucket compression itself:
the geometry concentrates tokens into a sublinear number of active sectors, so many explicit gate decisions become
redundant at the level of the present model family.
The current routing substrate is best described as a normalized angular manifold with Hopf-base sector
discretization. The implementation-level details evolved over multiple increments, but the essential logic is stable:
normalized tokens are mapped to angular sectors; those sectors define routing buckets; only the concentrated subset
of effective buckets is activated for downstream compute or memory access. A transport field can amplify the
effect, but transport does not replace the geometric source of sparsity. The strongest measured gains occur in the
TRANS regime, where transport acts as an amplifier on top of the angular manifold rather than as an independent
routing principle.

4. Formal statement of the frozen working law
The law frozen in the current repository is intentionally modest. It is not a universal claim about all geometric
routers. It is a canonical statement about the present router family under normalized embeddings and angular sector
discretization. In the strongest static regime studied so far, the effective routed footprint obeys:
 effective_buckets(K) = 2.957 * K^0.572
This law should be read together with two qualifications. First, the exact exponent depends on the regime. Internal
training experiments yielded an exponent near 0.50 in one range and nearer 0.64 in a larger-K training range, while
the static router law froze at 0.572. Second, what is invariant is not the exact decimal expansion but the sublinear
character of the footprint and its angular mechanism. For the current architecture family, the active footprint grows
substantially slower than K itself. That is the architectural fact with design consequences.
The corresponding mechanistic statement is: for normalized embeddings routed by Hopf-base angular sectors,
effective bucket occupancy grows sublinearly because the manifold organizes tokens into a concentrated subset of
angular sectors. The law is angular, not radial. Changing the norm family while preserving normalization leaves the
exponent essentially unchanged. Forcing radial shells does not improve the effect and can reduce concentration.

5. Experimental program and controls
The experimental program was intentionally incremental and adversarial. Earlier increments repeatedly showed
that pointwise distortion metrics were the wrong instrument. Per-sample MSE, error-based gating, and other local
fidelity metrics were repeatedly found to be geometry-agnostic under the EMA prototype proxy, because local
reconstruction quality could be equalized regardless of whether routing respected structure. The project therefore
pivoted from local-error observables to structural observables. This pivot matters because it explains why the later
results are coherent while the early MSE-based story was not.
The central controls are ORIG versus PERM and BASE versus TRANS. ORIG retains the geometry-native
organization; PERM acts as a permuted-routing control. BASE is the simpler routing mode; TRANS augments
routing with transport and consistently amplifies the effect. The important measurements in the stabilized protocol
are structural: bucket purity, bucket entropy, routing concentration (for example Gini coefficient and top-half
concentration), effective bucket count or routing perplexity, matched-progress routed cost, and hardware proxy
costs. These are the observables that survived the metric audit.
The project also used mechanism probes. A shell audit discovered that the routed tokens had r_eff = 1.0 because
the vectors were normalized. That eliminated a hidden source of ambiguity: shells were not merely inactive, they
were structurally absent in the default design. A norm audit then tested L1, L2, L3, and L4 normalization. A
shell-forcing audit tested whether introducing shell structure improved the routing law. It did not. These probes
matter because they turn the scaling observation into a mechanism explanation rather than a coincidence.

9. Matched-progress compute advantage and predictive closure
A scaling law is only interesting if it predicts something operational. The matched-progress experiments tested
whether ORIG reaches the same level of progress with less routed computation. Progress was aligned using a
checkpoint variable (cosine similarity) but the mechanism itself was always evaluated using routed cost, not cosine.
The critical result was that the measured compute advantage closely matched the prediction derived from the fitted
exponent difference. That is what gave the project mechanistic closure.

10. Hardware proxy closure
The next bridge was to test whether routing concentration actually translates into hardware-relevant savings under
simple proxy models. Three proxy families were evaluated at matched progress: cumulative effective bucket cost,
cache-line grouping, and finite-cache LRU miss proxies, along with a bytes-moved proxy. ORIG was cheaper than
PERM in every tested K value and in both modes. The strongest gains appeared in TRANS, where routing
concentration is highest.

11. Mechanism audit: norm invariance and shell non-necessity
The mechanism audit is where the architecture became simpler rather than more complicated. The critical
discovery was that the routed data lived on a normalized hypersphere. In the default router family, r_eff = 1.0 by
construction, so radial shells are absent. This means the earlier intuition about radial shell hierarchies, while useful
as an exploratory hypothesis, is not the operative source of the measured gains in the present architecture. The
gains come from angular sector allocation.

This audit has three consequences. First, shell logic should not be part of the default router design as long as

12. Canonical law and design implications
The current canonical law can therefore be stated compactly. For the present router family, normalized embeddings
are routed on a single-shell angular manifold, and the effective routed footprint grows sublinearly with nominal
bucket capacity according to a regime-dependent power law whose strongest static freeze is approximately 2.957 *
K^0.572. This law is norm-invariant across tested L1/L2/L3/L4 normalization families and is degraded rather than
improved by forced radial shells. The measured hardware advantage follows from this routing law through reduced
active bucket cost, fewer cache-line touches, fewer cache misses, and lower bytes moved.

In practical terms, the router should currently be built around normalized angular routing, increasing sector
resolution K and transport quality rather than around shell hierarchies or elaborate gate machinery. This is not a
philosophical preference; it is what the measured results support. If future experiments on full trainable models
reveal a need for richer radial structure, shells can be revisited. But the burden of proof has shifted. Shells are no
longer the default.
13. Limitations and unresolved questions
The present preprint is still an internal architecture report rather than a final external claim. Several limitations
matter. First, the results are presently derived from an internal routing-and-proxy program rather than from a full
large-language-model training run. The work has therefore established a routing law and a hardware-proxy bridge,
not a state-of-the-art language-model benchmark. Second, the law should be treated as a family law for the present
router implementation, not as a universal constant. The exponent varies somewhat by regime, even though the
mechanism is stable. Third, the hardware models are proxies. They are strong enough to motivate implementation,
but they are not replacements for actual kernel-level or device-level measurements. Fourth, the large-K regime still
needs explicit exploration to decide whether the sublinear advantage persists indefinitely, transitions to a new
regime, or saturates.
A fifth limitation is social rather than mathematical: geometric routing can be made to look more mystical than it is.
The current evidence does not require grand claims about brains or physics. The clean claim is already good
enough. A normalized angular manifold can compress routed computation into a sublinear effective footprint. That
alone would be an interesting architecture principle if it holds up in real model training.
14. Next steps
There are three immediate next steps. The first is a large-K angular capacity test to determine whether the sublinear
advantage continues, saturates, or changes regime at much larger routing resolutions. The second is integration into
a real trainable model so that the compute and hardware story can be tested under an end-to-end task rather than
only under the current routed proxy program. The third is systems work: if the angular router is going to matter in
practice, it should be married to an execution design that can exploit the locality benefits it appears to create.
A useful medium-term goal is to test whether the same angular law appears when routing is attached to
bucket-specific experts, retrieval memories, or hybrid expert-memory modules. The architecture already suggests
that many old design choices can be revisited under the new constraint: once the active routed footprint grows
sublinearly, the problem of how to fill those active buckets with useful compute becomes much more interesting
than the problem of how to gate them in the first place.
